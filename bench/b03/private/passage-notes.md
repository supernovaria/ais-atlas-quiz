## Discrimination pairs, and what each name gives away

- **Tharl vs wennet.** A tharl is fastened to rock at both ends; a wennet only to other threads. Neither name has an English meaning, so neither half is given away. The residual leak is the passage's own phrase "thick, taut threads" for beginners' tharls, which an outsider could map to "anchored".
- **Spur vs wennet (one thing).** A spur is just a wennet with both ends on one tharl. "Spur" (a short projecting part) suggests something short near a larger member, which fits the passage's description; it does not say whether the spur is a separate kind, which is the load-bearing point.
- **Vesk vs orra.** Vesks lay tharls, orras lay wennets; a vesk never lays wennets on its own lattice but may act as an orra elsewhere. Neither name means anything, so neither half is given away. The phases were renamed out of existence: the text says "before/after its last tharl is anchored", not "founding/keeping".
- **Cant vs pell.** Cant = wennets / tharls, whole lattice. Pell = wennets in the longest wennet-to-wennet chain, never along a tharl, no wennet twice. "Cant" means a tilt or jargon in English and "pell" nothing; neither points to "ratio" or "longest chain". Both rise while tharls are still being anchored; a high-cant lattice can have short pell.
- **Ostral vs calvine.** Ostral when pell > 2 x cant; calvine otherwise. No visual or English meaning, and the old "membrane vs net of ropes" description was removed, so the direction of the rule is no longer given by the words.
- **Tessel trace vs fenn trace.** Tessel = longest chain through the wennet nearest the middle; fenn = longest chain from the wennet nearest each anchor, best kept. Tessel is the better shortcut despite fewer starting points. Neither name says "middle" or "edge" (the old "centre/rim" did).
- **Orrin pattern vs Tasker pattern.** Same noun, so the noun gives nothing away. Orrin: compares lattices by how they were begun (several vesks -> shorter pell), colder galleries only. Tasker: one lattice through time (pell falls after the last tharl), draughty galleries only. The conditions are on different axes (temperature vs air movement), so one does not mirror the other.
- **Slackening vs the Tasker pattern (one thing).** Varrenby's "slackening" is the Tasker pattern; her mechanism (the air stretches chains) is false. The name "slackening" points toward her false mechanism, i.e. the wrong way.
- **Tamber vs pilse.** Tamber = gradual change in the mucus; pilse = wear by crawling snails. Neither name has an English meaning. The definitions in the text do describe each, so a stem that includes the definition gives the mechanism; the diagnostic (where the break falls) is not in the words.
- **Four objectors.** Tasker objected to the Orrin pattern (anchoring speed; overruled). Emmerick objected to the ostral/calvine boundary (carried into practice). Aske objected to Varrenby's counting order (carried). Pole objected to the tessel trace (conceded, practice unchanged). Tasker is also the name of a pattern: deliberate.

A reader with graph intuitions can reason about the definitions (for example that a spur must be a wennet, or that breaking a cut-vertex shortens the longest path), but not about any empirical claim. - Emmerick's objection (a category boundary that flips on one small change, fixed by averaging several values) is a generic brittleness-of-thresholds argument; his *proposal* follows from the objection and is tagged guessable (C37). That practice then adopted it is not guessable from the frame.
