# 4.3 The Architecture of Cord-Snail Lattices

In the flooded galleries of the Aumark karst, the cord-snail spends most of its adult life hanging from lattices of hardened mucus thread strung across the gallery ceilings, some a few hand-widths above the water and some close under the crown of the vault. A lattice accumulates over several seasons, the work of different animals doing different things. Its study has rested since Ilse Varrenby's survey of the 1950s on a small vocabulary. This section sets out that vocabulary, the two measures built from it and the two best-established regularities in the field, and then works through a single lattice.

## Tharls and wennets

Every thread in a lattice is one of two kinds. A **tharl** runs from one anchor point on the rock to another; it touches nothing but stone at its ends. A **wennet** runs from one thread to another and at no point touches the rock. A wennet may join two tharls, a tharl and a wennet, or two wennets. The distinction concerns what a thread is fastened to, not its length or thickness. Beginners classify by appearance, calling thick, taut threads tharls and fine, slack ones wennets. In most lattices this rule of thumb gives the right count; the Aumark field protocol still asks observers to follow every thread to both of its ends, because the lattices where it fails cannot be picked out in advance.

Older surveys recognised a third kind, the spur: a short thread hung near an anchor, with both of its ends on a single tharl. A spur is simply a wennet whose two ends happen to lie on the same thread, and the protocol now counts it as one, as beginners always had.

The two kinds are laid by different animals. A **vesk** is a snail that lays tharls; an **orra** is a snail that lays wennets. The roles do not mix within one lattice. An orra never lays a tharl, and a vesk never returns to lay wennets on a lattice it began, though it may act as an orra on a lattice begun by another snail. Orras do not wait for the frame to be finished. They begin laying wennets as soon as the first tharls are anchored, so most of a lattice's wennets are laid before its last tharl is in place. After that, only orras work.

## Cant and pell

Varrenby defined two measures, and the field still uses them in her form.

**Cant** is the number of wennets divided by the number of tharls. It is a ratio for the whole lattice and says nothing about where the wennets are.

**Pell** is found by tracing chains. Start at any wennet, move to a wennet fastened to it, then to one fastened to that, and so on, never travelling along a tharl and never passing through any wennet twice. Pell is the number of wennets in the longest such chain anywhere in the lattice. A lattice with a high cant can easily have a short pell if its many wennets each run directly from tharl to tharl.

The two measures are easy to conflate, because both rise while a lattice's tharls are still being anchored, and nearly all of a lattice's pell is laid in that period, but they answer different questions. Cant measures how much wennet-work a lattice has received relative to its frame; pell measures how far that work has become independent of the frame. Varrenby's classification uses both: a lattice is ostral when its pell is more than twice its cant, and calvine otherwise.

At any one time, most resting snails are found on lattices of middling pell, from about four to seven wennets, and lattices with a very short or a very long pell carry few, even when they are large.

In the field, wennets are counted before tharls. Varrenby traced the tharls first, and the order was reversed after Arnulf Aske showed what that costs: following a tharl to its anchors means bringing the lamp close to the rock, and the warmth slackens nearby wennets so that they sag against one another and can no longer be told apart.

When two observers count the same lattice independently, their tharl counts agree more closely than their wennet counts, and cant inherits most of its measurement error from the wennet count.

A full trace from every wennet is slow, and two shortcuts are in common use. The tessel trace finds the longest chain that passes through the wennet nearest the middle of the lattice. The fenn trace finds the longest chain beginning from the wennet nearest each anchor, and keeps the longest result. Beginners tend to prefer the fenn trace because it uses more starting points, but wennets near the anchors are mostly spurs, and chains begun there end quickly. The tessel trace underestimates pell less often, and by less, than the fenn trace does. Mira Pole objected that it can never find a longest chain that avoids the middle; the point is conceded, and the tessel trace remains the standard shortcut.

## Two regularities

The first is the Orrin pattern. Hallam Orrin, re-examining Varrenby's survey of 212 lattices, found that lattices begun by several vesks together had a shorter pell than lattices begun by a single vesk with the same number of tharls. The Orrin pattern appears only in the colder galleries; in the warmer ones, lattices begun by one vesk and by several have the same pell. Beatrix Tasker objected to Orrin's result on the ground that his multi-vesk lattices had been anchored more quickly, and so had received fewer seasons of wennet-laying before their last tharl was in place. Orrin answered by comparing lattices whose anchoring had taken the same number of seasons, and the difference persisted, which is why the pattern is now treated as established.

The second is the Tasker pattern, which concerns change over time rather than how a lattice was begun. In draughty galleries, a lattice's pell falls from season to season once its last tharl is anchored, while its tharl count stays fixed and its cant barely changes. In still galleries, pell after anchoring stays roughly level. Varrenby had noticed long chains vanishing in draughty galleries and described it as a separate process, slackening, in which the moving air stretches long chains until they part. Slackening is the Tasker pattern itself, and the air stretches nothing. Orras go on laying wennets and old wennets are lost at about the same rate, but the new wennets run more often from tharl to tharl than the ones they replace, so long chains are broken into short ones. The two regularities are not versions of one finding: the Orrin pattern compares lattices by how they were begun, while the Tasker pattern follows single lattices through time.

## Where lattices are found and how they fail

Lattices are most common in galleries of middling damp, and scarcer both in the driest galleries and in those whose air is saturated, although the snails themselves are no less numerous in the saturated galleries. Cold slows building: the colder the gallery, the more seasons a lattice takes to reach its full count of tharls.

The number of orras a lattice attracts has no bearing on its cant. Lattices worked by one orra and by six reach the same cant, because each orra lays fewer wennets when others share the lattice. Cant does bear on survival: the higher a lattice's cant, the more likely it is to come through a flood season intact, and Varrenby's figures show no level of cant beyond which the advantage stops growing.

Most lattices that are lost are lost to violence: a flood that scours the gallery, or a fall of rock that tears the anchors away. The higher a lattice hangs above the water, the less likely it is to survive a flood season, and those close under the crown are lost most often of all. Varrenby worried a great deal about silt film, the slow settling of fine sediment on the threads, but silt film accounts for only a small share of lost lattices.

Within lattices that survive, the pattern is different. Wennets that fail in a standing lattice fail mostly through tamber, a gradual change in the hardened mucus, rather than through pilse, the wear of snails crawling over them. Beginners put most broken wennets down to pilse. The reliable way to tell them apart is where the break falls: a wennet lost to tamber parts close to one of its fastenings, and a wennet lost to pilse parts near its middle.

## A worked example

Consider the Sellen lattice, in the fourth gallery of the Aumark system. Following the protocol, the observer first counts the wennets and finds 27, then traces the tharls to their anchors and finds 9. Cant is 27 divided by 9, which is 3.

For pell, a full trace finds that the longest chain runs through 7 wennets and passes through the wennet nearest the lattice's centre, so the tessel trace would also have found it. The fenn trace would not. The longest chain it finds from any wennet beside an anchor runs through 5.

Twice the cant is 6. The full-trace pell of 7 is more than 6, so by Varrenby's rule the Sellen lattice is ostral. Had the observer relied on the fenn trace, a pell of 5 would have made it calvine.

The example also shows the ground of Joost Emmerick's objection. Emmerick did not dispute Varrenby's definitions of cant or pell; he objected that the boundary between ostral and calvine makes a lattice's class depend on a single thread. Every chain in the Sellen lattice longer than 4 wennets passes through its centre wennet. If that one wennet were to break, the longest remaining chain would run through 4, and the lattice would be calvine, although its cant would have fallen only from 3 to 26 divided by 9, about 2.9. Emmerick proposed that the class be assigned from the mean length of the three longest chains that share no wennet, rather than from the single longest. For the Sellen lattice those chains run through 7, 4 and 3 wennets, a mean of about 4.7, less than twice its cant, which makes it calvine. Varrenby replied that a class which can change when one thread breaks is recording something real, namely that the lattice's long chains depend on a single line of wennets. Her reply did not carry the field. Since the 1970s most surveys have assigned the class from Emmerick's mean, keeping Varrenby's rule only for comparison with her own survey.
