# Undercraft Theory in Ensemble Performance Studies

## Two kinds of contribution

Ensemble craft studies is the branch of performance sociology concerned with
how small touring troupes divide labor during a live show. Teodor Kasch, who
founded the field with his 1958 study of the itinerant troupe he called the
Corvide Nine, argued that everything a performer does on stage falls into one
of two categories, and that collapsing the two together is the single most
common error made by outside observers.

**Projection** is the register of skill that reads across the full house:
voice carrying to the back row, gesture scaled to be legible at distance,
comic timing pitched to a crowd rather than a scene partner. Projection is,
by definition, what the audience is watching.

**Undercraft** is everything that holds a scene together without being
watched: tracking where a prop was left three scenes ago, adjusting spacing
so a slower partner isn't stranded downstage, absorbing a dropped line by
feeding the next cue before the silence becomes noticeable. Undercraft
succeeds, in Kasch's phrase, "by vanishing." A spectator who can describe an
undercraft action afterward is describing a *failure* of it.

Kasch's foundational claim is that these two categories are not two ends of
one scale. A performer's projection tells you how loud and legible they are.
It does not tell you, in either direction, how good their undercraft is — the
two are separately acquired, separately trained, and (this is the part later
researchers found hardest to accept) *negatively* associated in practice, not
merely uncorrelated.

## The Fen index

Kasch needed a way to score undercraft without relying on audience report,
since by definition a well-executed undercraft action is one the audience
never notices. His solution was to score it from backstage: a trained
observer logs every moment a scene required an uncredited correction —
a missed prop, a late entrance, a dropped line — and records whether the
correction succeeded without being visible to the house. The **Fen index**
for a performer, on a given production, is the proportion of *attempted*
corrections that succeeded invisibly:

> Fen index = (corrections that succeeded invisibly) ÷ (corrections attempted)

Two things follow directly from this definition that are easy to miss. First,
the Fen index says nothing about *how often* a performer needed to correct
anything — it is a rate among attempts, not a count. A performer who needed
one correction all night and made it cleanly scores the same 1.0 as a
performer who needed thirty and caught all thirty. Kasch called the raw count
of needed corrections the **correction load**, and treated it as a separate
quantity entirely. A troupe can carry a heroic Fen index while carrying a
crushing correction load, and Kasch regarded that combination — not a low
Fen index — as the more dangerous diagnostic picture, for reasons taken up
below.

## The halo fallacy

The naive assumption — call it the halo fallacy — is that the performer who
commands the audience's attention most completely is also the performer you'd
most trust to hold a scene together when something breaks. Kasch's backstage
logs said the opposite. Across the forty touring troupes in his original
survey, a performer's rank on projection and their rank on Fen index were
*negatively* associated (Kasch reported a rank correlation near −0.35,
offered only as a rough illustration of the direction, not a precise law).
His explanation was attentional, not moral: sustaining projection at full
scale narrows a performer's peripheral awareness of the rest of the stage.
The performer working hardest to be seen has the least attention left over
for the things they are not supposed to be seen doing.

## Casting the anchor

Every documented troupe structure, in Kasch's survey, includes a position he
called the **anchor**: the stage position nearest what he termed the sightline
dead zone, from which most undercraft actions originate — the place spacing
gets corrected from, the place a dropped prop is most often quietly retrieved.
The anchor position carries the highest correction load in the show and the
lowest visibility.

This produces the second finding that outside observers reliably get wrong.
Given that the anchor is structurally central to the show, an observer who
has not read Kasch's data will guess it goes to the strongest performer — the
lead. Kasch's troupes did the opposite with striking consistency: the anchor
was given to the troupe's *weakest projector*, and the strongest projector
was placed in the **lead** position, the spot with the highest projection
demand and the lowest correction load. The lead is cast for what the audience
sees; the anchor is cast for what the audience must not have to see. Treating
"anchor" as a synonym for "lead" — assuming the most structurally important
position is also the most prominent one — is, in Kasch's terms, exactly the
halo fallacy applied to blocking.

## The gloss effect

If projection and undercraft are separate axes, audience judgment tracks only
one of them, and tracks it in a way that can actively mislead. Kasch's
post-show surveys asked audiences to rate a production's "seamlessness" on a
five-point scale — a measure he called **gloss**. Gloss rises reliably with
a troupe's average projection quality. It does not reliably rise with the
troupe's Fen index, and across several of his multi-season case studies it
moved in the opposite direction: as a troupe's projection improved over a
touring season, audience-rated gloss climbed even as backstage logs showed
the Fen index quietly declining. Kasch's account is that a confident,
well-projected recovery from a visible stumble is often rated *higher* by
audiences than a scene that needed no recovery at all, because the audience
mistakes the confidence of the cover for the tightness of the craft. A rising
gloss score is therefore not evidence that a troupe's undercraft has
improved; it may be evidence of the opposite, dressed convincingly.

## Troupe size and the core band

A separate strand of Kasch's survey concerned troupe size. The intuitive
guess runs in one of two directions: either bigger troupes have more hands
available to cover for each other and should show higher Fen indices, or
smaller troupes coordinate more tightly and should show higher Fen indices.
Kasch found neither monotonic pattern. Average Fen index rose with troupe
size up to a size he called the **core band** — five to seven performers —
and *fell* on both sides of it, for two structurally different reasons.
Troupes smaller than four lacked redundancy: when one performer was pulled
into heavy projection, there was often no one free to cover the resulting
gap. Troupes larger than ten suffered a different failure: undercraft depends
on quiet, fast, often nonverbal signaling between whichever performers are
free at a given moment, and above roughly ten performers the number of
possible signaling pairs grows fast enough that cues were missed or
misdirected even when plenty of people were, in principle, available to give
them. The core band is the size range where redundancy is available but the
signaling network is still small enough to stay legible.

## Rescue, repair, and the correction load

Kasch distinguished two ways a troupe reduces its correction load's damage,
and warned that conflating them was the most consequential mistake a touring
manager could make. A **rescue** is a live, in-performance correction — the
kind the Fen index scores. A **repair** is a change made *between*
performances, in rehearsal, that removes the underlying cause so the same
correction never needs to be attempted again — resetting a prop table,
re-blocking a transition, adjusting a cue that was ambiguous.

A troupe can have an excellent Fen index built entirely out of rescues and
no repairs at all: the same three problems recur every night, and the troupe
catches all three every night. Kasch treated a high rescue rate combined with
a flat or growing correction load — rather than a low Fen index — as the
clearest warning sign in his data, because it meant the troupe had gotten
skilled at surviving its own unfixed mistakes rather than fixing them. A
troupe with a lower Fen index but a shrinking correction load, by contrast,
was one whose repairs were steadily working, even though its raw invisible-
correction success rate looked worse on paper.

## Ferrant's objection

Kasch's chief critic, Odalys Ferrant, did not dispute the projection–undercraft
distinction but attacked how "invisible" was measured. Ferrant argued that
backstage observers were classifying two audience responses as one: a
correction the audience truly never registered, and a correction the audience
noticed but *forgave* — folding it into their enjoyment as a charming save
rather than a flaw. Ferrant's 1971 survey of twelve troupes found that only
three kept rehearsal logs detailed enough to separate the two cases at all,
a number she offered mainly to illustrate how rarely the distinction was even
tracked. Kasch's partial concession was a revised protocol, exit
questionnaires designed to catch "noticed-but-forgiven" separately from true
non-detection, though no published survey has yet used it at scale — so the
scale of the conflation Ferrant identified remains, by Kasch's own admission,
unmeasured.
