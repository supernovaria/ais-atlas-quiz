# Notes on `passage.md` (fabricated control passage)

Domain: an invented sociology-of-performance field ("undercraft theory")
about how touring stage troupes divide labor into audience-facing skill
("projection") and invisible structural skill ("undercraft"). Not AI, not
computing, not forecasting. No named term overlaps with the existing
tier4-control passage (Vantine / korrel / lattice drift / Brenmark are not
used).

## (a) Counterintuitive claims and the "obvious wrong answer" for each

1. **Projection and Fen index (undercraft success) are negatively
   associated, not positively.**
   Obvious wrong answer: the most commanding/watchable performer is also the
   most reliable one when something breaks backstage (a halo-effect guess).
   The passage states the correlation runs the other way and gives an
   attentional mechanism (projection narrows peripheral awareness).

2. **The anchor position — the structurally most important spot for
   undercraft — is given to the weakest projector, not the star.**
   Obvious wrong answer: the most critical position on stage goes to the
   lead/strongest performer. The passage explicitly reverses this and
   explains why (anchor = high correction load, low visibility; lead = high
   projection demand, low correction load).

3. **Audience-rated "gloss" (seamlessness) can rise while the real Fen
   index falls — a visible, confident recovery is often rated *higher* than
   a scene that needed no recovery at all.**
   Obvious wrong answer: audience satisfaction with seamlessness tracks
   actual technical execution, so rising acclaim implies improving
   undercraft. The passage says the two can move in opposite directions.

4. **Troupe size has a non-monotonic effect on Fen index (peaks at a
   mid-sized "core band" of 5–7), and the two failure modes on either side
   are mechanistically different from each other.**
   Obvious wrong answer: either "bigger is always better" (more hands to
   cover) or "smaller is always better" (tighter coordination) — a single
   monotonic story in either direction. The passage rejects both and gives
   two distinct causal mechanisms (redundancy scarcity below the band,
   signaling-network overload above it).

5. **A high rescue rate combined with a flat/growing correction load is a
   warning sign, not a mark of skill — sometimes worse than a lower Fen
   index paired with a shrinking correction load.**
   Obvious wrong answer: a troupe that successfully rescues everything
   (high Fen index) is doing well, full stop. The passage states that an
   excellent Fen index built entirely from rescues with zero repairs means
   the same problems recur nightly and are merely being survived, which
   Kasch treats as the more dangerous diagnostic picture.

(That's five; the brief asked for at least four.)

## (b) Discrimination pairs (concepts a careless reader would conflate)

1. **Projection vs. undercraft** — audience-facing expressive skill vs.
   invisible structural skill. Careless reading: "more skilled" as one
   undifferentiated scale. Stated distinction: separately acquired,
   separately trained, and negatively associated in the data.

2. **Rescue vs. repair** — a live in-performance correction (scored by the
   Fen index) vs. a between-performance structural fix that removes the
   need for the correction going forward. Careless reading: both are just
   "fixing problems." Stated distinction: rescues can pile up indefinitely
   without ever becoming repairs, and that combination is diagnosed as
   unhealthy.

3. **Fen index vs. correction load** — a *rate* among attempted corrections
   vs. the raw *count* of corrections needed at all. Careless reading:
   a high Fen index means "few problems." Stated distinction: a performer
   who needed one correction and made it scores identically (1.0) to one
   who needed thirty and caught all thirty; load and rate are independent.

4. **Fen index vs. gloss** — the backstage-logged technical success rate
   vs. the audience-reported seamlessness rating. Careless reading:
   audience approval is a proxy for technical quality. Stated distinction:
   the two can diverge, and in Kasch's case studies sometimes moved in
   opposite directions across a season.

5. **Anchor vs. lead** — the position with the highest correction load and
   lowest visibility vs. the position with the highest projection demand
   and lowest correction load. Careless reading: "anchor" sounds central/
   important so it should be the star's spot. Stated distinction: casting
   is inverted relative to that intuition.

(Five pairs given; the brief asked for at least three.)

## (c) Honest assessment: is any of this a thin renaming of a real concept?

- The **projection/undercraft split** is loosely evocative of a real,
  informal distinction performers and directors do talk about (stage
  presence vs. stagecraft, or "front of house" skill vs. technical
  reliability), but there is no codified academic theory, named quantity,
  or named researcher in real performance studies that this maps onto
  one-to-one. I don't think this voids the control, but I'll flag it as
  the least novel piece of the framework — a reader with theater
  experience might find the *category split itself* unsurprising, even
  though every specific claim built on top of it (the negative
  correlation, the anchor-casting reversal, the gloss effect, the
  non-monotonic troupe-size effect, the rescue/repair distinction) is
  invented and not derivable from general theater knowledge.
- The **Fen index formula** (successes ÷ attempts) is a generic
  ratio-of-successes-to-attempts construction, structurally similar to
  things like a save percentage or a batting-average-style statistic in
  sports. That structural shape is common enough (not tied to one specific
  real theory) that I don't think it constitutes "a real framework in
  disguise" — there's no equivalent named "Fen index" or claim set in real
  sports statistics that the passage's substantive claims are secretly
  importing.
- The **core-band (non-monotonic troupe-size) effect** is the piece most
  likely to trigger an "oh, this is just [X]" reflex — inverse-U /
  Goldilocks effects are extremely common across many real domains (team
  size research, Yerkes-Dodson arousal-performance curves, etc.). I judged
  this an acceptable risk rather than a fatal one: an inverse-U *shape* is
  a generic mathematical pattern, not a specific theory, and I made the
  two failure mechanisms on either side of the band domain-specific
  (redundancy scarcity vs. signaling-network overload) rather than
  reusing any specific real theory's mechanism. But if this control comes
  back with an anomalously high adversary score, the core-band section is
  where I'd suspect leakage first, precisely because "quality peaks at a
  medium size/dose/group-size" is a pattern a test-wise guesser could
  apply as a heuristic even without having read the passage.
- Nothing else in the passage (Fen index vs. correction load, rescue vs.
  repair, the gloss effect, anchor/lead casting) has an existing named
  real-world counterpart that I'm aware of; the researchers (Kasch,
  Ferrant), the institution-free framing, the troupe name (Corvide Nine),
  and all numbers are invented for this file.
