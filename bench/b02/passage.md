# Reading the Serrow Cairns: Register, Cant and Tithe

## The cairn as a text

The marker cairns of the Serrow uplands were built to be read, not merely seen. A walker who has only ever used cairns as a line of dots across a hillside will notice that a Serrow cairn is there. A walker trained in the upland convention will also learn from it which way the route turns, whether the ford below is passable, and sometimes that a junction lies ahead. This section introduces the framework that the study of these cairns has settled on, the two quantities surveyors measure, and the main disputes about what a cairn says.

A Serrow cairn has three parts. The footing is the bottom course, laid directly on the ground. Above it sit the body courses, usually between three and nine of them. On top is the crown, a single flat stone. On many cairns a small loose stone rests on the crown. This loose stone is called the tithe stone, because every walker who passes is expected to give it a moment's attention.

## Two registers: laid sense and worn sense

The organising idea of the field comes from Idris Marhaug, whose 1968 monograph *The Standing Stone and the Passing Hand* argued that a cairn carries two separate registers of meaning. The laid sense is whatever the builder put into the arrangement of the body courses, and it does not change unless the cairn is rebuilt. The worn sense is carried by the tithe stone alone. A passer who has just crossed the ford turns the stone to the upslope side of the crown if the water was low and to the downslope side if it was not. The worn sense is therefore an accumulated record of what recent walkers found, and it changes from week to week.

The two registers answer different questions. The laid sense says where the route goes; the worn sense says what state the route is in. It is a common beginner's error to treat the tithe stone as a direction marker, and an equally common one to treat the body courses as news.

Marhaug's scheme also names the cairns that carry only one register. A blind cairn has canted body courses but no tithe stone, so it has a laid sense and no worn sense. A dumb cairn has a tithe stone but no deliberate cant in its body courses, so it has a worn sense and no laid sense.

Not everyone accepted the two-register model. Cormac Brannagh objected that the worn sense is not independent of the builder, because the builder sets the tithe stone's first position, its seed position, and passers on quiet routes rarely move it. On his account, much of what Marhaug called worn sense is really laid sense that happens to sit on the crown. The objection is still discussed, but surveyors continue to record the two registers separately.

## Lean and cant

Two quantities are measured on every cairn, and they are easily confused. Lean is the tilt of the cairn as a whole away from the vertical. It is almost always produced by frost heave under the footing, and it carries no meaning. Cant is the deliberate offset of a single body course relative to the course beneath it. The builder sets each course a finger's width to the east or to the west of the one below, and the direction of the offset points the way the route turns after the cairn.

Because cant is defined relative to the course below, a cairn's lean does not alter its cant at all. Lean matters only to readers who judge cant by eye against the vertical rather than course against course. That habit belongs to experienced readers more than to strangers: locals learn to glance at the silhouette of a cairn, while strangers, lacking the habit, compare each stone with the one beneath it. Lean misleads experienced readers more often than it misleads strangers.

The Serrow survey protocol fixes the order of work. The tithe stone's position is recorded first, before anyone touches the cairn, since measuring the courses almost always knocks it. Lean is taken next, with a plumb line. Cant is then measured course by course from the crown downward. Nothing in the stone requires this direction; Marhaug's recording sheets were printed that way, and later surveyors kept the order so that their figures could be compared with his.

## The concord index

Marhaug summarised the laid sense of a cairn in a single number, the concord index. It is the share of adjacent pairs of courses whose cants point the same way. The footing is excluded, since it follows the slope of the ground and has no cant of its own; the crown is included and counted as the uppermost course. A cairn whose every course is canted east has a concord of 1.

Oda Tessaly objected to including the crown, on the grounds that passers often reset the crown when they turn the tithe stone, so its cant records handling rather than building. The standard index nonetheless includes it, and Tessaly's own survey reports it that way.

Concord matters chiefly to strangers. In Tessaly's survey, strangers read high-concord cairns more reliably than low-concord ones, and the relation held across the whole range, with no point beyond which more concord stopped helping. Local walkers read cairns equally well whatever their concord.

A low concord is not always noise. Builders of the upland guild mark a junction ahead with a cant-break: a single course canted against the courses above and below it. A cant-break does not give the route direction; it announces that a second route branches off before the next cairn. Because guild builders use cant-breaks and shepherds building for their own flocks do not, shepherds' cairns have a higher concord on average than guild cairns.

## A worked example

Consider a cairn on the eastern approach to the Oskel Pass with a footing, five body courses and a crown, and a tithe stone on the downslope side of the crown. The crown is canted east, and so are body courses five and four. Course three is canted west. Courses two and one are canted east.

Setting the footing aside, the cairn has six courses and therefore five adjacent pairs. The pairs formed by courses one and two, four and five, and five and the crown agree; the pairs two and three, and three and four, disagree. The concord is three in five, or 0.6.

A careless reading takes the west cant of course three as the message, because it is the odd one out. A trained reader sees a single reversed course flanked by agreeing ones, which is the form of a cant-break. The route turns east after this cairn, and a branch leaves it before the next one. The tithe stone belongs to the other register entirely. It says nothing about direction; it says that recent walkers found the ford high.

## When the registers disagree

Tessaly's 1983 survey of the Oskel Pass route recorded 412 cairns, of which 71 showed a conflict between laid and worn sense: the courses pointed down to the ford while the tithe stone said the ford was high. Local walkers facing such a cairn almost always followed the laid sense. Tessaly called this habit shadowing, since the older register casts its shadow over the newer one, and the effect now bears her name. In 58 of the 71 conflicts the worn sense was the correct one, so Tessaly shadowing is a costly habit rather than a wise one.

Tessaly shadowing must not be confused with Orne inversion, described by Lise Orne in 1990. Tessaly shadowing concerns experienced readers and a conflict between the two registers. Orne inversion concerns strangers and the body courses alone: a stranger who meets a cant-break reads the reversed course as the route direction, and so turns the wrong way. Orne inversion is rarer when the next cairn is already in view, because the stranger can check the line.

How reliable the worn sense is depends on traffic. On routes with only a few passers a month the tithe stone goes stale, and on the busiest routes it is turned by walkers who do not know the convention. Worn sense is most reliable on routes of moderate traffic. The weight of the tithe stone behaves in the same way. A very light stone is shifted by wind and sheep, a very heavy one is not turned at all, and stones of middling weight give the most faithful record.

Height, by contrast, shows no such pattern. Marhaug predicted that cairns of four to six body courses would be read best, reasoning that shorter cairns carry too few cants and taller ones too many to follow. Tessaly found that strangers misread cairns at the same rate whatever their height. Above the fifth body course builders rarely cant at all, and the extra courses are ballast added for visibility.

## Sources of error

Surveyors have tried to rank the hazards a walker faces. In Tessaly's survey, local walkers misrouted at about 4 per cent of cairns and strangers at about 19 per cent. Among strangers, Orne inversion accounted for more misroutings than collapsed cairns, which strangers generally recognise as unreadable and ignore. False crowns, loose stones placed on blind cairns by visitors who have seen tithe stones elsewhere, caused fewer misroutings than ordinary frost lean.

Restoration is a hazard of a different kind. Conservators who rebuild a fallen cairn tend to lay every course with the same cant, which raises its concord and erases any cant-break, and they set the tithe stone back in its seed position. A restored cairn is easier for a stranger to read, and it says less than the cairn it replaced.
